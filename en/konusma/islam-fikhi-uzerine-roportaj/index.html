An Interview on Islamic Fiqh with Mehmet Çelen

About 17 min read

Translated from the Turkish original.Read the original

Q. 1. Sir, if you wish, let us begin first with a general assessment of the work done on Islamic fiqh in Turkey, both in academic circles and in unofficial circles. How do you assess the work done on Islamic fiqh in Turkey?

Mehmet Çelen: In the last 30 years in particular a great deal of work has been done on Islamic fiqh. Whether in academic circles or in unofficial circles, original studies have been made on the main subjects of fiqh. What is pleasing is that many of these original studies have been published. They have taken the place of translated works. These works, without remaining tied to a single school of fiqh, have also reflected the views of many mujtahids and have tried to answer the needs of our day. For example, just as the basic questions of Islamic law, the principles of usul al-fiqh and basic criminal law have been made into books, so along with them a number of questions of detail have been too. Our belief that in the future these studies will acquire the quality of being able to find solutions to all the problems of the Muslims is greater and stronger than it was yesterday.

Q. 2. As far as we know, you too have been working on Islamic fiqh for a long time. Would you tell us about the work you have done?

Mehmet Çelen: For my own part, I have been carrying on studies in fiqh since I was small. Starting from those days I took many lessons, first of all on the detailed questions of fiqh, on usul al-fiqh, on the differences of opinion among the jurists and on the proofs they brought for their views, and my research on this subject still continues. Most recently, praise be to Allah, we had the opportunity to publish our study entitled Adultery and Stoning in Islamic Law. In the future, if we are able to complete our work on lessons in Islamic law and on fiqh and life, we are thinking, God willing, of publishing it.

Q. 3. Sir, speaking for Turkey, there are many questions that we live with and meet, and they need to be solved according to Islamic fiqh. At the very least the Muslims need to have an Islamic point of view on these questions. For example, sales on deferred payment, transactions involving interest, insurance, the stock exchange, the status of industrial foods and the like. From time to time some scholarly boards hold meetings on these. Or views on these questions emerge through some individual studies or through translations. The question is this: are these questions that are to be settled by individual ijtihads? Or how far is it possible for the boards in question to represent all Muslims? To what degree are they binding? How and by what are the qualifications of those who take part in such boards determined?

Mehmet Çelen: As for the first sentence of your question, there are two understandings on this subject in Turkey. One says: these questions do not concern us. The other says: we have to take an interest in the questions of the society in which we live and find solutions to them. We prefer the second view. Wherever a Muslim lives, he has to solve and resolve his own problems and the responsibilities laid upon him in the society in which he lives, or his responsibility as an individual, according to the principles of Islamic law. And for this, to enter into intellectual effort, to think, to research and to establish the principles that are right is our duty in the first degree. Before coming to individual ijtihad, we have to say that it is not possible to find solutions to the questions of our day with translated works. They can only shed light for us and show us the way. What is fundamental in ijtihad, in our opinion, is individual and living ijtihad. There is no such thing as ijtihad by a board. This means that individual ijtihads have, in all questions, a determining, resolving and binding quality. As for the representative capacity of ijtihads, they are in a position that binds only those who follow the person who made the ijtihad. For those outside that, there is no question of a binding quality. In the present picture in Turkey, since the representation of those who take part in such boards is not fully inclusive, it is not possible to determine qualifications at a level that would satisfy. Still, scholarly personalities who have distinguished themselves in their subjects and fields naturally take their place on such boards. But to determine the criteria for taking part in these boards and to conduct affairs within their framework does not seem possible for the present. I do find it useful, however, to state that I am of the opinion that these things will come about in the future.

Q. 4. Another question connected with this: let us say, does holding an academic title in the relevant field of theology have a side that is binding on Muslims?

Mehmet Çelen: Holding certain academic titles in the faculties of theology does not give that person the quality of an Islamic scholar or of a mujtahid. For that reason bindingness is possible only through being an Islamic scholar. Otherwise, in this matter, merely holding an academic title, being a researcher and writer, or having had a madrasa education is not enough.

Q. 5. In that case, who are the Islamic scholars, and how shall we recognise them?

Mehmet Çelen: The Islamic scholar is hidden in the purport of the expression "dhikr" in the verse of the Qur'an: "If you do not know, ask the people of the dhikr." (al-Anbiya': 7) Just as dhikr includes reflection, thinking and the activities of the mind, it also expresses, in the direction of piety (taqwa), keeping Allah constantly in mind and acting upon the remembrance of Allah when ordering one's life. A person who cannot think, who cannot put his power of reasoning to work, who cannot grasp the subtleties and depths of questions and who cannot make full use of his mental faculties can by no means be an Islamic scholar. A person who has no piety, who does not live the rulings of Islam in his life, who does not do righteous deeds and who plunges into sin and immorality cannot acquire the quality of an Islamic scholar either. A person who approves of un-Islamic systems, takes his place at their side, supports their views and practices that are contrary to Islam and is in the service of the unbelievers does not have the identity of an Islamic scholar, whatever his title may be. As heir of the Prophet, to follow the way of the Messenger is the first quality of the Islamic scholar. Only those who fear Allah and have piety are Islamic scholars. For "Of His servants, only those who have knowledge fear Allah." (Fatir: 28) Thus knowledge, the highest of ranks, becomes one with faith. Within the framework of these measures we can easily understand and recognise who the Islamic scholar is and who it ought to be.

Q. 6. Is there a possibility of forming scholarly boards in order to settle our social questions, or at least of forming an ijma' for this geography? How do you assess it?

Mehmet Çelen: It is always possible to form scholarly boards in our country. But up to the present day formations of this kind have not been able to bring about ijma'. For ijma' to be formed, in our opinion, an assembly or a society of scholars must be set up, and this formation must embrace all sections. The work of such a society may in the future create the ground on which ijma' can be formed across the geography. And for such a thing to come about, everyone who says "I am a Muslim" must on no account withhold his material and moral support for this formation at the highest level.

Q. 7. Well, in our day there is a setting in which specialisation has a particular importance. In this case, what can be done about the people of fiqh seeking solutions to questions by consulting, in matters on which ijtihad is made and in which they are not specialists, Muslim men of learning who are specialists in that matter?

Mehmet Çelen: In fact the matter of specialisation in particular fields is a subject still debated in scholarly circles. Especially in a situation in which blind specialisation is often met with, the fact that specialisation alone is not enough of course makes consultation and the exchange of ideas with the other branches of knowledge necessary. In any case the jurists are bound to express their ijtihad only after duly carrying out all the work they need to do in the field in which they can make ijtihad and after putting forth all their effort. This too makes very frequent consultations among the people of knowledge necessary. As a result of these consultations and of being informed, the people of fiqh can put forward their own views. The realisation of this is possible through the society of scholars, which we touched on above, being brought into operation as soon as possible.

Q. 8. Do the fatwas that issue from the religious institutions set up by un-Islamic systems, even if there are truly learned Muslims in them, have any binding force from the point of view of Islamic fiqh?

Mehmet Çelen: The fatwas that issue from the religious institutions set up by un-Islamic systems are, from the point of view of Muslims, invalid, void and meaningless. For here there is a problem of legitimacy. The non-Muslims in power have never set up these institutions so that Muslims should practise their beliefs and their acts of worship according to the Qur'an and the sound Sunna. Their one and only aim is, by means of these institutions, to keep the Muslims and Islamic work under their control and to prevent them from presenting a situation that could be dangerous for themselves. It is, by means of these institutions, to turn the transformations and changes of society to their own advantage. As is stated in a verse: "Never will the Jews or the Christians be pleased with you until you follow their religion." (al-Baqara: 120) Moreover, the fatwa given by a person who serves the unbelievers and takes his place in their ranks is also invalid and void from the point of view of Islamic fiqh. It has no side that is binding on other Muslims either. Here too it emerges that the non-Muslims aim, with these institutions they have set up, to be able to manage the Muslims easily under their own hegemony. And for this they have never shrunk from spending billions of dollars from the budget. In addition, these institutions carry out work to cloud minds in order to prevent the true Islamic belief and thought from gaining currency among the people. For example, when the Soviet Union occupied Afghanistan and the Muslims rose up to defend their country, to fight against the occupying forces and to drive the occupiers out of the country, the council of scholars that was in Afghanistan and was attached to the government issued a fatwa, with no fear of Allah and without scruple, to the effect that the Muslims who were waging jihad in the way of Allah should be fought and that jihad should be waged against them. That is why institutions and fatwas of this kind have no side that is binding on Muslims.

Q. 9. In Turkey, as in the whole Islamic world, there are different lines, from the various schools of the tradition to the modernist approach. How are these approaches and lines reflected in the solving of the questions of Islamic fiqh? How shall we find the middle way that we need? Also, in a setting in which the tradition of the ijaza in the Islamic sciences is not possible outside madrasa-style education, how will it be possible to obtain ijma' in social questions? Are the means of the official educational institutions sufficient to give the qualification relating to the Islamic sciences?

Mehmet Çelen: The reflection of such different schools on Islamic fiqh takes place, in reality, in a negative way on the whole. At the root of this negativity lie bigotry and the disease of putting one's own school before everything. For this reason a middle way cannot be found. In our day there are some positive developments, for example the conference of Islamic scholars and congresses on fiqh, hadith, tafsir and so on. Work of this kind sheds light for us on the point that in the future many questions will be resolved by decisions to be taken in common by Muslim scholars. Such work should be supported materially and morally and increased. The means of the official institutions are of course not sufficient on the point of giving the necessary qualification relating to the Islamic sciences. For studying knowledge in depth is not possible there. But Muslim individuals can, by making use of these means too, educate themselves, develop themselves and come to have a say. Thus, by distinguishing themselves in society through their knowledge, they can represent themselves in the place where they ought to be. At the first symposium on Islamic thought held in Turkey in the nineties, in our paper on ijtihad, we had a proposal for the opening of an "Institute of Ijtihad". But up to today there has been no development. Of course matters such as these are a matter of need. If Allah grants the means, we shall work for such an institute to come to life. For this will be a means for the raising of master personalities and mujtahids in the Islamic sciences and in their own fields. Islamic society has a very serious need of this in order to form and light up its future. It must also not be forgotten that the natural leaders of Islamic society are the scholars. Of course, for the whole umma to mobilise as one body for the raising of scholars is among the greatest and foremost of the duties that fall upon it.

Q. 10. Let us also ask this in connection with tradition: at one time a number of our writers, for the most part, starting from certain understandings and practices of the people that are described as traditional, entered into a criticism of tradition. As a result we saw periods in which a coldness towards our traditional heritage was experienced. There are two problems here. One is the severing, by official means, of the living bond with tradition together with the Republic, the efforts to tear the people away from their historical veins and, connected with this, the fact that the official (from our point of view natural) traditional line that lives in our minds is on the point of being lost. The second, again connected with this, is the confusions of concepts that have formed in our minds. One of these concepts is tradition: what should be meant when one says a sound tradition?

Mehmet Çelen: Before coming to the answer to this question, we need to dwell on tradition and on some concepts connected with it. These are, together with tradition (gelenek), the concepts of custom (görenek), 'urf and habit ('ada). These concepts are often confused with one another and wrongly used in one another's place. Yet there are very important nuances between them. For example, while tradition is everything that is the result of the accumulation of culture formed over time in a society, görenek expresses the habits that are done by having seen them from those of old and that are formed as a result of trial. While we call tradition an'ane, we can call görenek i'tiyad and 'ada. 'Ada is the thing that, by being done and done again, practised and practised again, becomes a natural behaviour and is observed by everyone. 'Urf is different: this word, which is Arabic, takes within its scope the good and right behaviours that find acceptance in society and are observed by everyone. The word ma'ruf, which expresses goodness and everything that accords with reason and religion, is also from this root. Thus, within the content of the concept of 'urf there is no question of a behaviour that is evil, wrong, ugly or contrary to reason and religion. That is why rulings such as "'Urf is given the force of law" and "What is established by 'urf is established by religion" and their like are found in the Mecelle. There is no such privilege for habits and customs. Among these there may be bad or good, right or wrong.

That is why, when one says the tradition of Islam or Islamic tradition, the first thing that comes to mind is walking on a right line. It is to live the basic principles of Islam, to preserve the Islamic spirit and to continue the Islamic sense of things from the last Prophet to our day. And this is the tradition of the people of the Sunna, and it is to follow the way of the righteous predecessors (al-salaf al-salih). It is to go in the footsteps of, first of all, the Book of Allah, the Qur'an, of the established sound Sunna practices of the Messenger Muhammad (peace and blessings be upon him), its interpreter and the one who applied it, of the acts of the noble Companions, of the methods of those who followed them well, of the way of the mujtahid imams, and of the method of the Islamic scholars who have continued this tradition. It is this chain that forms the structure of sound tradition. With us, especially in our last fifty years, sound tradition and the bad habits and customs of the people were for the most part confused with each other. The main principles that have come down through the centuries and the innovations (bid'a), habits and bad customs formed later were all run together, and a criticism of tradition was made. This in turn caused right and wrong, truth and falsehood, to be mixed with each other. As a result of this a wholesale coldness towards our heritage of tradition came about. The way to remove this, of course, is for what is right and what is wrong to be distinguished, and for what sound tradition is to be stated and explained openly and in a manner that leaves no room for doubt.

The idea that the bond of our society with tradition was officially severed with the Republic is in fact an incomplete finding and diagnosis. It will, I believe, be more correct for us to say that the act of severing and distancing the nation of Islam from tradition was begun from the last two hundred years of the Ottomans. The fact that Westernised minds were in very important places among the Ottomans made this process still quicker and easier. The movements of reform, the Tanzimat and the innovations all ended, in the name of improvement, in declaring war on tradition and in Westernisation. In any case Western modernism, everywhere it went, began its work first of all by razing to the ground whatever there was in the name of tradition. And in doing so it made no distinction between right and wrong, good and bad. This continued in a swifter and more rapid way with the Republic. For this reason, when examining events, we need to look at questions from many sides.

Sir, we thank you for the valuable information you have given and wish you success in your work.

Mehmet Çelen: We too thank you very much and convey our greetings to your readers.
