Our Ancient Cities of Learning
(4-11 June 2025) (1)

This year, June 2025 was a very important time for me personally. On 4 June 2025 I entered Uzbekistan by road, through Afghanistan's land border at Hairatan. I set out for Termez and from there for Samarkand in order to follow the traces of the past, to breathe history in by living it, to take it down from the dusty shelves and bring it through the tunnel of time to our own day, and to visit on the spot the works of famous statesmen and scholars. This journey enriched me greatly.

After these conquests the cities of Bukhara, Samarkand and Termez in particular became centres of learning.

After meeting our family at Samarkand International Airport we went to the flat we had rented and rested that night. On 5 June we began to walk around Samarkand. In the opening of this article I naturally wish to pass on what we witnessed and observed in these three ancient and historic cities.

The points I shall dwell on here are sociological, psychological, political and economic observations. It can be seen that Uzbekistan, which gained its independence after the collapse of the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics (USSR) in December 1991, has come a long way. In history these regions are known as Khorasan or Transoxiana (Mawarannahr). The conquests that began in particular in the time of the third caliph, Uthman (may Allah be pleased with him), and continued under the Umayyads brought these regions into the religion of Islam. After these conquests the cities of Bukhara, Samarkand and Termez in particular became centres of learning. Seekers of knowledge flocked to these cities from all the surrounding countries in order to study.

Many scholars who led the nation of Islam came from this region. Seen from that side, these are very fertile lands. What made this possible, of course, were the heads of state and statesmen who valued learning and the advance of learning. The names mentioned first are Imam al-Maturidi, deeply versed in the sciences of creed and kalam and of Qur'anic exegesis; in jurisprudence al-Marghinani and al-Qaffal al-Shashi; in hadith Imam al-Bukhari, Imam al-Darimi (this hadith scholar is also called al-Samarqandi) and Imam al-Tirmidhi; in exegesis al-Nasafi and al-Zamakhshari; in Sufism, among the seven masters of Bukhara, Shah Baha' al-Din Muhammad Naqshband, Abd al-Khaliq al-Ghijduwani al-Malati and others; and from Termez al-Hakim al-Tirmidhi and Abdullah Ahrar.

From the sociological side, one can always observe that the authoritarian structure left over from the past continues. To see that the traffic lights are not violated even at four in the morning, a very deserted hour of the night, at a moment when nobody is around, is both pleasing and thought-provoking. In general the working of society is carried on in order and good arrangement. The city planning is well placed and very fine. The design and outward look of the workplaces recall the historic buildings and are in harmony and accord. This gives one a sense of ease. Inside the cities the roads are very wide, built like motorways with three lanes in each direction. The pavements are wide, orderly and clean, at the service of pedestrians. The general state of building that I saw in the three cities reflects a horizontal architecture. There are no skyscrapers or very tall buildings. This kind of architecture gives one peace and ease. The roads between the cities, on the other hand, have not been developed; they are laid out as asphalt roads with one lane in each direction. In some places, however, these roads are in bad repair and inadequate. This of course requires drivers who travel overland between the cities to drive more carefully.

Apart from small places, alcohol is sold in almost all the large supermarkets. Many of the restaurants and cafés are the same. This shows that the consumption of alcohol is at a very high level. The free sale of intoxicants, the mother of all evils, puts the future of society in danger. Nevertheless we can state that on the avenues and streets we did not see people who disturb others because of drink. It is certainly our right to say that this, too, is pleasing. It must of course be granted that in a period as short as seven days it is quite hard to establish many things.

Mosques Are Open

Uzbekistan is a society that is 85 % Muslim. Of course its having lived for long years under the rule of the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics (USSR) caused certain disruptions and shortcomings in religious life. For the Soviet regime, being an atheist and socialist regime, put religions and religious life under pressure. The other republics, certainly, were affected very badly by this state of affairs.

Here we witnessed that the mosques are open and that the five daily prayers are performed. I too performed the daily prayers there; the congregation makes two or three rows. Some young people also come to the daily prayers. One cannot help being glad of it. However, the Friday and festival prayers are not performed in every mosque. Certain mosques have been set aside for these prayers. On the first day of the Feast of Sacrifice, after performing the dawn prayer, I went to perform the festival prayer. There was nobody in the mosque. I looked right and left and searched for a notice, but did not come across one either. Then a young man came. A few more Muslims came, and they too were surprised. We saw that nobody was coming or going, and the time of the festival prayer that we had been told had passed. I asked myself what to do, and said to the young man there, let us perform the festival prayer here. He agreed, and so we performed the festival prayer with a small congregation. Later we went to the same mosque for the night prayer; after the prayer I asked the imam why they had not hung up a notice saying that the festival and Friday prayers are not performed here, and he said that they had. But we had not been able to see anything. So someone must have taken it down. The call to prayer is recited inside over a loudspeaker, but its sound is hardly heard outside. We witnessed that there is such a practice here. Had we been able to meet an official, I would have tried to learn the reason. We performed the Friday prayer in a historic mosque named Ruhabad, built in the 18th century, beside the mausoleum of Amir Timur. Although the mosque is small, hundreds of Muslims performed the Friday prayer outside, in the park. Young people and our children were at this prayer too.

Dress

Above all, when you say that you have come from Turkey, they are sure to welcome you with a smiling face and to ask how you are.

In more modest places and in the markets one can come across local dress. In the city centres, however, Western styles of dress prevail. Women who wear miniskirts are quite few. Perhaps these are tourists from abroad. We did not see a single Muslim woman wearing a face veil. As we learned afterwards, we read the news that wearing the face veil is forbidden and that those who wear it are fined more than 250 USD.1 The people are very calm and they also treat tourists well. Above all, when you say that you have come from Turkey, they are sure to welcome you with a smiling face and to ask how you are. We met with this very often and were happy. It made us very happy indeed. The people are also helpful and warm. When you ask for an address, they try to help you in a way you can understand.

Trade, Shopping and Beggars

Here one should also look at the way trade works in daily life. In supermarkets, shopping centres and restaurants, whatever the price is, that is what you are charged. We do not meet with the greed of taking more money from you because you are a tourist. The markets work in the same way. At tourist sites, however, they quote high prices. That, certainly, is not a pleasant thing. For example you can do 300,000 som worth of shopping for 200,000 som. So one has to watch out for this when shopping at tourist sites. The currency of Uzbekistan is the som. 1 USD is 13,000 som (04.06.2025); this is of course approximate. Small changes in the exchange rate can be seen from day to day.

On the avenues and streets and at the junctions where traffic is heavy it is not possible to see a beggar. For the cities here this is a pleasing and very fine thing. At tourist sites you may come across one here and there. These too do not behave in a way that would disturb people.

Safe Cities

I felt as if I were walking around Istanbul.

Samarkand, Bukhara and Termez are truly safe cities. I felt as if I were walking around Istanbul. I did not feel a stranger at all. I have visited dozens of cities; I had this feeling once in Beirut, and the other time in these three cities. Truly these four cities were ancient and historic centres of learning. By night and by day we completed our tour in great comfort, without feeling any fear and without anyone following us. These cities were safe at night too. I thank the civil authorities of those cities for having secured this and offer them my congratulations.

Transport

Transport is quite easy too. We made our trips by taxi. The prices are reasonable as well. The city buses run regularly; the fare is 3,000 som. You can pay in cash or buy a ticket. Among taxis one should prefer not the independent ones but the taxis that work with the Yandex app. For the others generally ask twice as much, although the destination is the same. Travelling by train between the cities is pleasant too. There is a high-speed train between Samarkand and Bukhara. Between Termez and Samarkand there is an ordinary train, but it takes about 9 hours. There is also a train between Samarkand and Tashkent. When one is going by train it is better to buy the ticket online beforehand.

Faith Tourism

Our journey to three cities of Uzbekistan also brought out certain observations of ours about faith tourism. It caught the eye especially when we visited in Bukhara the tombs of the great Sufis known as the seven masters. Just as in Turkey the imperial mosques are visited, especially in Ramadan, so here too these tombs were being visited by the local people, who came by bus. That the tombs have been built up and, with buildings such as a prayer hall, a library and a museum placed within them, have the look of a complex increased this still more. The present administration was encouraging it too. The high entrance fee charged in particular at historic buildings and museums (for those who are not Uzbek citizens) did not exist at these tombs. Entrance to all of these tombs and complexes was free. People were coming, praying and walking through every part of the complex. We too, of course, visited these religious and historic places in detail.

The Empire of Amir Timur

Amir Timur was born on 8 April 1336 in the village of Khoja Ilgar near Shahrisabz. A great commander and statesman, Amir Timur gained mastery of Transoxiana by his strategic and tactical manoeuvres and ascended the throne in Samarkand in 1370. That he is called "Timur the Lame" (Aksak Timur) or "Tamerlane" (Timurlenk) is due to his having been wounded in his right arm and right leg during this period of struggle. Although he was of Mongol descent, Amir Timur did not use the titles "Khan" and "Khaqan"; instead he preferred the term "Amir", which was widespread in the Islamic world.

During his reign Amir Timur took important parts of Iran, Iraq, Syria, Anatolia, Azerbaijan and India and founded a great empire. He also defeated the Ottoman sultan Bayezid the Thunderbolt in the battle fought on 28 July 1402 in the Çubuk valley near Ankara, and took possession of Anatolia.

The most magnificent of the buildings that Timur had built in Samarkand under his own personal care is the Mosque of Samarkand, called the Bibi-Khanym Mosque. Another of his great buildings in Samarkand is the Blue Palace (Gök Saray), which was used mostly for keeping the state treasury. It was also Timur who had built the mausoleum, the "Gur-e Amir", in which he himself and some members of the dynasty are buried. Another of the most important works he had built is the lodge (khanqah) of Ahmad Yasawi.

Amir Timur even launched a campaign against China. He declared that he did so with the aim of dealing the polytheists a heavy blow. Leaving Samarkand on 27 November 1404, he crossed the river Syr Darya over the ice and reached Otrar, but there he fell ill, died on 18 February 1405 and was buried in his mausoleum in Samarkand. May Allah have mercy on him.

During the 35 years of his rule Amir Timur applied the rulings of Islam and built many madrasas, mosques and centres of learning, above all in the capital Samarkand, and in Bukhara and other cities. He set great store by learning, art and scholars. He turned Samarkand and Bukhara into centres of learning. Many of the historic monuments found in Uzbekistan today come from his time. All of them reflect the architecture of Central Asia.

The City of Samarkand

It was Amir Timur who made Samarkand a great and historic city. For years Samarkand was famous as the capital of the Timurid Empire. Registan Square, in the centre of the city, comprises three madrasas laid out in the shape of a U and a mosque. The mausoleum and museum of Amir Timur, called the Gur-e Amir, are also in this city. The most magnificent of the buildings that Timur had built under his own personal care, for his wife, is the Mosque of Samarkand, called the Bibi-Khanym Mosque. The Ulugh Beg Observatory is likewise in Samarkand. The tomb and mosque of the great scholar Imam al-Maturidi are in Samarkand too. The great hadith scholar Imam al-Bukhari is also buried in Samarkand. It will be fitting for us to give detailed explanations of the monuments found in Samarkand.

Registan Square

Registan Square, in the city of Samarkand, was for long years the heart of the city. This square is also known as a political square where the rulers' decrees were read out and death sentences were carried out. In this form it was at the same time the square of politics in history. Amir Timur displayed in this square the spoils he won on his campaigns and held important ceremonies here. On this square there are three madrasas and also the Registan Mosque. Today, moreover, seating has been arranged here for watching, as in a theatre. People sit here and watch animations, visual shows and concerts, and the square hosts festivals. This square lies on the Silk Road, and its name means "Sandy Place".

aa- The Ulugh Beg Madrasa

It was built between 1417 and 1420 by Ulugh Beg, the famous scholar of mathematics and astronomy and grandson of Amir Timur. Built on the model of Central Asian architecture, this madrasa served the advance of learning for long years. It is also known as the School of Astronomy and Mathematics of Samarkand. It is the first madrasa built on Registan Square. Ulugh Beg himself also taught in his own madrasa. As one of the greatest astronomers and mathematicians of that age, Ulugh Beg is both a scholar and a statesman. He is at the same time the fourth sultan of the Timurid Empire.

In the middle of the madrasa there is a large hall, and there are 80 rooms in which great numbers of students lived. In each corner there is a classroom. Today souvenirs are sold in these rooms. Above the doors there are verses of the Qur'an and various narrations written on turquoise tiles and mosaics. The purpose of these narrations is to give messages to the students who studied there. All of them are short words of wisdom that encourage learning and praise scholarly work. Some of the inscriptions that have stayed in my memory are these: "The seeker of the path of knowledge is a seeker of Paradise." "Truly the scholars are the heirs of the prophets." "The scholars of my community are like the prophets of the Children of Israel." "The beauty of the servant lies only in faith." There are verses, hadiths and wise sayings like these.

ab- The Sher-Dor Madrasa

This madrasa was built between 1619 and 1636 by Yalangtush Bahadur, the governor of Samarkand; it takes its name from the two lion figures on the upper right and left of the entrance gate. In Persian, Sher-Dor means "Lion Gate". In this madrasa too the Asian architecture and style prevail. It draws attention with its entrance portal with a pointed arch. Here too there are verses and various narrations written on turquoise tiles and mosaics. For a long time this madrasa provided the education of philosophers and theologians.

ac- The Tilla-Kari Madrasa

The Tilla-Kari Madrasa stands on Registan Square. This madrasa was built between 1646 and 1660 on the order of the governor Yalangtush Bahadur Khan. It was used both as a centre of education and as a mosque. The mosque inside the madrasa is decorated with motifs worked in gold and with gilded reliefs, and many verses and narrations are written in it.

In Persian the madrasa here is called the "Gold-Worked / Gilded Madrasa". The dome of the madrasa is decorated with tree-of-life ornaments. The mosque here is also given the name "Registan Mosque". Its pulpit is made of stone, and the prayer niche is decorated with stone carving. At present no prayers are performed inside this mosque. In the inner part of the mosque there are some souvenir sellers.

This madrasa and mosque present themselves as the third building on Registan Square.

The Mausoleum/Tomb of Amir Timur (Gur-e Amir)

This mausoleum was built by the family of Amir Timur and completed in 1408. It was originally first built for Amir Timur's grandson. It houses the bodies of Timur's sons and grandsons. After Amir Timur died in 1405, he too was buried here. After Amir Timur was buried here it was given the name "Gur-e Amir". This mausoleum bears traces of distinctive painting and ornament that represent Mughal (Baburid) architecture. With its blue dome and its minarets this monument has a magnificent appearance. The verses of the Qur'an and the noble hadiths worked into the walls are dazzling as well.

In this mausoleum the bodies of Amir Timur and of his grandson Muhammad Sultan lie side by side. The grave of his other grandson, Ulugh Beg, is here too. The graves of his sons Shah Rukh and Miran Shah are also inside this mausoleum. The grave of Sayyid Baraka, the teacher whom Amir Timur greatly revered, is here as well.

The Ulugh Beg Observatory

Another important monument in the city of Samarkand was the Ulugh Beg Observatory, built in 1421. It is also given the name Samarkand Observatory or Ali Qushji Observatory.2 It was very important in that it reflected the science of astronomy of that time. The basic views held today in the science of astrology had been put forward by Ulugh Beg with his ideas at that time. Ulugh Beg was the most famous scholar of his age in astronomy, mathematics and the natural sciences. He was also famous as the amir who lit up the sky and the ruler who conquered the heavens. He has also been called the wise ruler.

Ulugh Beg is the fourth sultan of the Timurid Empire (1447-1449). His father is Shah Rukh, Timur's youngest son, and his mother is Gawhar Shad Begum. He was born on 22 March 1394 in the city of Soltaniyeh in Azerbaijan and died on 31 August 1449 in Samarkand. His grave is inside the Mausoleum of Amir Timur. During his own rule Samarkand lived through the most splendid days of the transmitted and the rational sciences.3

He himself had this observatory set up on a hill in Samarkand. The observatory, which remained active for thirty years until the death of Ulugh Beg, and the astronomical tables drawn up here were influential in the world of learning until the invention of the telescope. The famous scholar of mathematics and astronomy Ali Qushji also worked here. In order to remove certain errors of measurement and shortcomings that he saw in the Zij-i Ilkhani, which he was using, Ulugh Beg produced the Zij-i Ulugh Beg, which is accepted as a source work in its field both in the Islamic world and in Europe. It is also known that he carried out research in geometry, especially on triangles, and drew up tables of tangents and sines.4 When one visits this place, the Zij-i Ulugh Beg can be seen. We were able to look for quite a while at the mathematical symbols and astronomical values in it.

The Planetarium Museum of Astronomy

The Planetarium Museum of Astronomy, with the works exhibited inside the Ulugh Beg Observatory, holds a great deal of interesting material, from portraits of astronomers to pictures of the stars. Ulugh Beg is a famous scholar who made great contributions to the world of astronomical science. He also contributed to very important developments of Uzbekistan in the field of astronomy and to its progress in the science of astronomy. In this museum there are very special star catalogues that remain from the age of Ulugh Beg.

Both with its atmosphere and with the catalogues it presents, the Planetarium Museum of Astronomy promises an interesting experience in the field of astronomy.

The Afrasiyab Museum

The Afrasiyab Museum, inside Samarkand, is one of the largest archaeological sites in the world. Afrasiyab covers an area of 200 hectares in all. It lies quite close to the Bibi-Khanym Mosque. It is a large site where historic objects are exhibited in an ancient atmosphere. This place, which was home to different civilisations over the centuries, was destroyed at the beginning of the 13th century, in the time of Genghis Khan, as a result of the Mongol invasions.

Ferdowsi describes this place in detail in his work the Shahnameh. The objects found here are presented to visitors within a museum. It is said that the museum holds 22 thousand objects in all from antiquity.

The Samarkand Museum

The Samarkand Museum, one of the most popular museums of Uzbekistan, stands on the huge archaeological site known as Afrasiyab. A great many objects that remain from Afrasiyab, which was destroyed by the Mongols in the 1200s, are under protection in this museum. The building of the Samarkand Museum, located in the northern part of Samarkand, is the work of the Armenian architect Baghdasar Arzumanyan.

The museum, which was opened as part of the celebrations of the 2500th anniversary of the founding of the city, is divided into five separate rooms that hold traces of different periods. Today there are about 22 thousand objects in the museum in all. The Azerbaijan Pavilion, added here in 2015 as a result of cultural work carried out together with Azerbaijan, forms one of the most interesting parts of the museum.

g- The Bibi-Khanym Mosque and Madrasa

For Amir Timur had this place built as a mosque, and keeping this memory of his alive is the greatest respect that can be shown to him.

This mosque and madrasa is the place that Amir Timur had built in honour of his wife. Bibi Khatun, or Bibi Khanym, is Melike Hanım, daughter of the Chagatai khan Kazan Halil. This building, which with the largest portal in Samarkand has great dimensions and splendour, was built between 1399 and 1404. The work was designed as a complex, both mosque and madrasa. The dome of the building, which was put up immediately after the victory won by Amir Timur in India, is more than 40 metres high. In the courtyard, at the entrance, there is also a Qur'an stand made of stone.

This special building, in the centre of the city of Samarkand, has faced a great many disasters in the course of history. The Bibi-Khanym Mosque, regarded as a symbol of power, is within walking distance of Registan Square. Today it no longer serves as an active mosque but simply as a museum. When we looked inside the mosque, however, there were no tiles on the walls, and the floor was bare earth. Presumably it will be restored in the future. In fact, after repair and renovation the mosque ought to be returned to its original state as a place where prayers are performed. For Amir Timur had this place built as a mosque, and keeping this memory of his alive is the greatest respect that can be shown to him. There too, unfortunately, the rooms of the madrasa have been turned into souvenir shops.

h- Siyob Bazaar

The Siyob Bazaar in Samarkand is beside the Bibi-Khanym Mosque. It is also known as the open people's market. It is a huge market of 5 hectares that both local people and tourists visit and shop in. There are also coffee houses, restaurants and day traders here. The market offers every kind of foodstuff, fresh and dried fruit, textiles and household goods. We too bought shawls, textiles and dried fruit here. In the restaurants here one can get to know the local dishes. On the stalls, moreover, local products are sold in abundance.

Here too the sellers quote high prices, especially to tourists. Although we do not like it, we had to bargain when we shopped. There were no sale prices at all on the products and goods here. The authorities of this region could certainly make it compulsory to display the sale price and, by carrying out inspections, prevent excessive prices.

i- The Tomb of Imam al-Bukhari (may Allah have mercy on him)

Imam al-Bukhari was born in the city of Bukhara in the year 194 of the Hijra, on 20 July 810 CE. Al-Bukhari received this name because he was born in Bukhara. From the age of 10 he took lessons in the noble hadith from the hadith scholars of Bukhara. At the age of 16 he went to Mecca and continued these studies there. Later he carried on this journey of learning by continuing to study hadith in cities such as Medina, Egypt, Baghdad, Basra, Damascus (Sham), the Hijaz, Homs, Kufa, Merv and Balkh. After these journeys in search of knowledge he wrote his famous work named al-Jami' al-Sahih. In the Islamic world this work stands at the head of the hadith books known as "al-Kutub al-Sitta / the Six Books of hadith".

He fell ill in the small town of Khartank, 24 km from Samarkand. He died on the night of the Feast of Ramadan in the year 256 of the Hijra and was laid to rest there the next day, Friday 1 September 870.

The Tomb of Imam al-Bukhari is at present being renewed in a large restoration project. One can see that, following the restoration work carried out, sections such as offices, a mosque, a research centre and a library have been added to the site. In a way these works recall a great complex. This complex, built jointly by master craftsmen from different cities, covers an area of 10 hectares in all. Because of this restoration work, visits are not permitted at present.

k- Shah-i-Zinda / the Living Sultan

These are the tombs of the reign of Amir Timur, dazzling as works of art in turquoise and gold. In fact this place may also be called a special cemetery. As a result of the building of many tombs with portals and domes here, it turned into a cemetery quite unlike any other. In this cemetery is the grave of Qutham ibn Abbas (may Allah be pleased with him), one of the noble Companions. The Uzbeks have given this place the name Shah-i-Zinda, the living sultan. Because of this Companion, many notable persons have been buried here. The grave of this Companion is, in the spiritual sense, a very great grave; as one climbs towards the hilltop, it is the grave at the very top. Right beside the grave a room of a few square metres with a single door draws attention. It is said that a Muslim who visited the grave of the Companion Qutham ibn Abbas (may Allah be pleased with him) would enter this room of repentance and repent.

Qutham ibn Abbas (may Allah be pleased with him) is the son of Abbas ibn Abd al-Muttalib, the uncle of our Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him). His mother, Umm al-Fadl Lubaba bint al-Harith al-Hilaliyya, is the first woman to become a Muslim after Khadija and the sister of Maymuna, one of the wives of the Noble Messenger.

In the cemetery there we went into almost every tomb and took our photographs. But it will be useful to include here the observations of my esteemed teacher Bünyamin about a grave that escaped our notice. This is a tomb that Timur had built in the 14th century over the grave of his sister Shirin Bika Aga, who died in 1375. What makes this tomb important and different is that the wise words of the great Greek philosopher Socrates, which speak of life and death and describe man's relation to this world and the hereafter, are written in exquisite thuluth script, beginning at the lower right of the portal, turning across the top and ending at the lower left. A few of those wise words, which begin with "Qala Suqrat" (Socrates said), are these: "Man suffers torment in this world in all his states. I am amazed at one who seeks the world while death is at his heels. I am amazed, too, at one who builds a palace although his true home is his grave. The grave is a door, and everyone will enter it..." To write, or to have written, these wise words of Socrates on the portal of a tomb, when there are thousands of verses and hadiths in the Qur'an and the Sunna, is in fact a clear sign of the self-confidence of our civilisation. In keeping with the noble hadith of the Messenger of Allah (peace and blessings be upon him), "Wisdom is the lost property of the believer; wherever he finds it, he has the best right to take it"5, these wise words have adorned a whole great grave.6

l- The Mosque and Tomb of Imam al-Maturidi (may Allah have mercy on him)

The mosque and tomb of Imam al-Maturidi (may Allah have mercy on him) are 2 to 3 km from the Shah-i-Zinda cemetery. Leaving Shah-i-Zinda, one can also go to this mosque on foot. When you ask there, people show you the way. In this respect the Uzbek people, young and old, are very helpful; I congratulate them. We too, after visiting the Shah-i-Zinda cemetery, walked as far as the mosque. The Mosque of Imam al-Maturidi was modest and plain. In the middle of the mosque was the grave of Imam al-Maturidi. It too stood there in a plain manner. The tombs of the Naqshbandi sheikhs that we saw in Bukhara, for example, were more splendid and showy.

Imam al-Maturidi (may Allah have mercy on him) was born in 852-3 CE and died in Samarkand in 944 CE at the age of 92. He was also buried in the same city. He is a great scholar of kalam and creed and at the same time a great exegete. He is, moreover, the imam of the Maturidi school. The Maturidi school, which belongs to Ahl al-Sunna wa'l-Jama'a, is a school of kalam that keeps to the middle way and strikes a balance between reason and transmitted knowledge. He wrote a commentary of great value in the history of Qur'anic exegesis in Islam.

His Important Work:

Ta'wilat al-Qur'an (a book of exegesis). Ta'wilat al-Qur'an, a commentary of great value, has also been translated into Turkish in 18 volumes.

m- The Tomb of Ubaydullah Ahrar

Ubaydullah Ahrar is one of the great Sufis of the city of Samarkand, and his tomb too ought to be visited. His name is Ubaydullah ibn Mahmud ibn Shihab al-Din, and because he was born in Tashkent, the capital of Uzbekistan, he has also been called al-Tashkandi. In the Naqshbandi tradition he is known as Khwaja Ahrar, and he is one of the great saints of Turkestan. He is at the same time a Naqshbandi sheikh. Ubaydullah Ahrar spent his whole life calling people to Islam, guiding the people and doing good works.

His works:

  1. Fiqarat,
  2. Risala-i Walidiyya,
  3. Murasalat (Ruq'at), which consists of his letters,
  4. Risala-i Hawra'iyya (Sharh-i Hawra'iyya).

He passed to the abode of eternity in Samarkand at the age of 89, in the year 895 of the Hijra, 1490 CE. His body was buried in the Khwaja Kafshir quarter of Samarkand.7

o- The Amu Darya River

The total length of the Amu Darya, counted among the greatest rivers of Central Asia, is about 2600 km. In our country this river is known by the name Ceyhun (Jayhun). The river, which has two separate main branches, flows along the south of Surkhandarya. We crossed the Amu Darya as we passed from Afghanistan into Uzbekistan. As far as we could see, it was very wide.

After its long journey the Amu Darya flows into lands that belong to Turkmenistan; in Uzbekistan it passes through the regions of Bukhara and Khwarazm. This long river, which also passes through countries such as Afghanistan and Tajikistan, finally empties into the Aral Sea. The natural beauties around the river are used for activities such as nature walks, picnics and hiking.
